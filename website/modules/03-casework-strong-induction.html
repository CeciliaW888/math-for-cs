Day 3: Casework and Strong Induction

Merge sort and quicksort split a problem into pieces of unpredictable size. To prove algorithms like these correct, you need two tools: splitting into cases, and an induction that can lean on any smaller case.

Proof by cases

"C OR NOT C" is a tautology. So if P holds when C is true and when C is false, then P always holds. With more cases, you must also show they are exhaustive.

Six people: 3 mutual friends or 3 mutual strangers

0
Pick anyone, P

P has 5 relationships. Each one is either friend or stranger, never both.

1
Case 1: P has at least 3 friends (Q, R, S)

1A: If any two of Q, R, S are friends, those two plus P make a friend triangle. 1B: Otherwise, Q, R, S are mutual strangers.

2
Case 2: P has at most 2 friends

Then P has at least 3 strangers. This is Case 1 with the words "friend" and "stranger" swapped.

✓
Exhaustive

The number of friends is either at least 3 or at most 2, so no situation is missed. This number, 6, is the Ramsey number R(3,3).

⚠️
Missing cases sink proofs: Kempe's 1879 "proof" of the Four Color Theorem had a gap that went unnoticed for 11 years. Today the theorem's proof is fully machine-checked.

Strong induction: cooking from a stocked fridge

Ordinary induction lets you use only yesterday's leftovers, P(n − 1). Strong induction lets you use any dish made so far: P(0), …, P(n − 1).

I have a stack of 5 blocks. Splitting a stack into a and b scores a × b points. How do I maximise my score?
Split 2 + 3: 6 points. Then 1 + 2 + 1 more. Total 10.
Peel one block at a time: 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10. Same total?!
Guess: every strategy scores n(n − 1)/2. The first split makes stacks a and b, both smaller than n, of any size.
Strong induction says those stacks score a(a − 1)/2 and b(b − 1)/2. Add ab, and the algebra gives n(n − 1)/2. Proved.
💡
Key Insight: Reach for strong induction whenever size n breaks into smaller pieces whose sizes you can't predict. Each piece only needs to be smaller than n.

A strong-induction proof, run as code

A beat ordering always exists in a round-robin tournament. The proof splits the teams around a pivot, much like quicksort.

beat_order.py 
def beat_order(teams, beats):
    if len(teams) <= 1:
        return list(teams)
    t, rest = teams[0], teams[1:]
    winners = [s for s in rest if (s, t) in beats]
    losers = [s for s in rest if (t, s) in beats]
    return beat_order(winners, beats) + [t] + beat_order(losers, beats)
Goal: line up the teams so each one beat the next
Base cases: zero or one team is already a valid line-up
(nothing to check)
Choose a pivot team T. Every other team either beat T or lost to T
W = the teams that beat T, fewer than n of them
L = the teams T beat, also fewer than n
Strong induction orders W and L. The last team in W beat T, and T beat the first team in L
